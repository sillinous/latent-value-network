The Latent Value Network

10 Provocations That
Need Your Mind

The hardest problems in cooperative infrastructure, framed as debates. Pick one. Take a side. Change ours.

Each provocation has a live debate thread. Join the discussions on GitHub →

The LVN initiative has produced a white paper, an evidence base, and a protocol specification. But specifications don't solve problems — people do. These ten provocations represent the questions where we need the most help, framed as genuine debates where reasonable people can disagree. Each one could change the architecture. We're looking for the strongest arguments, not consensus.

01
Should the embedding model be centralized or federated?
AI/MLCryptographyGovernance

The Signal Exchange Protocol converts private context into abstract signal vectors using a learned embedding model. But who trains and controls that model determines who controls what "complementarity" means — and therefore who gets matched with whom.

At stake: Whether a small number of model trainers effectively decide what kinds of cooperation the network promotes, creating a subtle but powerful form of centralized control.
Pos ACentralize it. A single, publicly audited foundation model ensures consistent signal spaces across communities, simplifies interoperability, and can be optimized globally. Publish the model weights and training data for transparency.
Pos BFederate it. Each community trains on local cooperative data. This preserves sovereignty and lets different cultures define complementarity differently. Accept the translation cost at boundaries.
Pos CNeither — use a marketplace. Multiple competing embedding models. Agents choose which to use. Market dynamics converge toward the best models. But: does this just recreate the platform capture problem?
What's the failure mode you're most worried about — and which position best defends against it?
02
Is privacy-preserving semantic matching actually possible?
CryptographyAI/ML

Zero-knowledge proofs work beautifully for exact attribute matching ("prove you're over 18"). But the LVN needs fuzzy, semantic matching ("find someone whose situation complements mine in ways I can't articulate"). Can this be done under encryption with current techniques?

At stake: If the answer is no, the entire privacy architecture may need to accept some plaintext exposure. How much, and what are the acceptable tradeoffs?
Pos AYes, through homomorphic embedding comparison. Recent FHE advances allow approximate nearest-neighbor search on encrypted vectors. It's slow but viable. The latency is acceptable for non-real-time matching.
Pos BNo — accept minimal plaintext exposure. The signal vectors are already abstract enough to be non-identifying. Don't add cryptographic complexity that makes the system impractical. The Bloom filters provide plausible deniability.
Pos CSplit the problem. Pre-filter with encrypted exact matching (ZK), then do semantic comparison in a trusted execution environment (TEE). Different threat models for different phases.
What's the minimum privacy guarantee that makes the system trustworthy enough for adoption?
03
Does ambient matching inevitably become surveillance?
EthicsSocial SciencePolicy

The LVN's core promise is matching based on context people are already sharing with their AI assistant. But a system that understands your needs well enough to match you is also a system that understands your vulnerabilities well enough to exploit you. Is there a meaningful distinction?

At stake: Whether the entire premise of ambient matching is ethically defensible — or whether it's a surveillance system with good intentions, which history shows always gets captured.
Pos AThe distinction is architectural. In the LVN, context never leaves the user's device. The AI agent is a fiduciary acting in the user's interest. This is fundamentally different from centralized data collection. Architecture, not policy, is what prevents surveillance.
Pos BThe distinction is illusory. Any system that models your needs creates a targeting surface. The Signal Exchange Protocol, even with privacy protections, emits information about you. Given enough emissions, an adversary can build a profile. We should be honest about this risk.
Pos CThe question is comparative. People already share far more with Google, Meta, and Amazon than the LVN would ever process. The relevant question isn't "is this perfect?" but "is this better than the status quo?" If it provides cooperative value while leaking less than existing systems, it's a net improvement.
What would convince a privacy maximalist that this system is worth the risk?
04
How do you prevent the network from only serving the already-connected?
EthicsEconomicsCommunity Practice

The Chetty et al. data shows that the people who most benefit from cross-boundary connections are the least likely to have them. The LVN promises to fix this — but technology adoption follows power law distributions. The early adopters will be educated, tech-savvy, already-networked people.

At stake: Whether the LVN becomes another tool that advantages the advantaged, dressed up in equity language — or whether it can genuinely reach the people who need cooperative infrastructure most.
Pos ABuild community intermediaries into the protocol. Libraries, churches, mutual aid networks, social workers — institutions that already serve underconnected populations can operate agents on their behalf. This is E2 (Intermediary Support) and it must be first-class, not an afterthought.
Pos BStart where the pain is worst. Pilot in communities with the highest latent value gap — rural areas, immigrant communities, post-industrial towns. If the system proves its value there first, adoption follows naturally. Don't start in San Francisco.
Pos CBe honest: technology can't solve structural inequality. The LVN can make cooperation easier for people who already want to cooperate. Pretending it will reach the most marginalized creates false promises. Focus on making a good tool and let organizers decide how to deploy it.
Is equity-by-design a real architectural property, or a marketing claim that protocol design can't actually deliver?
05
Should matches include a price?
EconomicsEthicsSocial Science

The white paper commits to "pluralism of value" — accommodating gifts, reciprocity, barter, and transactions. But when a match surfaces between a retired accountant and a freelancer who needs tax help, who decides whether this is a favor, a mentorship, or a $200 consultation?

At stake: Whether introducing price mechanisms into cooperative matching fundamentally changes the nature of the cooperation — and whether NOT having prices creates exploitation where one party gives more than they intended.
Pos ADefault to no price, with opt-in monetization. Most valuable cooperation happens outside market logic. Adding prices by default turns the LVN into another gig platform. Let people negotiate terms after matching, outside the protocol.
Pos BInclude suggested value signals. People need a frame of reference. Without any value signal, the person with more power always extracts more. A "suggested exchange" (time, skill, or monetary) makes interactions more equitable, not less.
Pos CUse reciprocity credits. Create a non-monetary value token that tracks contribution without creating a market. Help someone → earn credit → spend credit on receiving help. Avoids both exploitation and commodification.
Think of the best cooperation you've ever experienced. Would it have happened if a price were attached?
06
Can you bootstrap trust without bootstrapping bias?
Social ScienceNetwork ScienceEthics

The Trust & Reputation Fabric (Layer 4) flows trust through relational chains. But relational trust networks are homophilous — people trust people like themselves. If trust bootstraps from existing social networks, it inherits their biases.

At stake: Whether the trust system becomes a mechanism that reproduces existing social stratification — "people like us trust people like us" — defeating the cross-boundary matching that makes the LVN valuable.
Pos AStart from institutional trust. Verified community organizations (libraries, clinics, churches) serve as trust anchors. Their endorsement bridges social boundaries because institutions serve diverse populations.
Pos BBuild trust through small acts. New participants start with low-stakes cooperative interactions and build trust through demonstrated reliability. The system observes behavior, not social connections. This is slower but more equitable.
Pos CAccept imperfect trust initially. Some risk is necessary for cooperation. Lower the trust threshold for cross-boundary matches and accept that some will fail. The alternative — requiring high trust for every interaction — perpetuates exactly the boundary-reinforcement the LVN aims to overcome.
What's the smallest viable unit of trust — the interaction that proves "I can cooperate with this person" at minimal risk?
07
Is "protocol, not platform" realistic or naive?
Systems ArchitectureEconomicsPolicy

The LVN insists it's a protocol layer, not a platform. But protocols need implementations, implementations need infrastructure, infrastructure needs funding, and funding creates power. Email is a protocol; Gmail dominates it. HTTP is a protocol; a handful of companies control the web.

At stake: Whether the "protocol not platform" framing is a genuine architectural commitment or a fig leaf that delays the inevitable platform capture by whoever operates the largest implementation.
Pos AProtocol-level anti-capture is possible. Design the protocol so that no single implementation can gain network effects that lock others out. Mandate interoperability. Make relay infrastructure commodity. This is hard but not impossible — ActivityPub (Mastodon) demonstrates it.
Pos BBuild the platform, but make it a cooperative. Accept that someone will build the dominant implementation. Make sure it's a cooperative owned by its participants, not a VC-backed startup. Protocol purity is less important than governance structure.
Pos CStart as a protocol, knowing it'll become a platform, and design the transition. Build capture-resistance into the protocol but plan explicitly for the scenario where one implementation dominates. Pre-commit to governance handoffs. Design the exit ramp before you need it.
Name a protocol that successfully resisted platform capture at scale. What made it work?
08
What if AI assistants don't want to cooperate?
AI/MLSystems ArchitectureEconomics

The LVN assumes AI assistants will implement cooperative protocols. But Claude is built by Anthropic. GPT by OpenAI. Gemini by Google. These companies compete. Their incentive is to keep user context proprietary — it's their moat. Why would they implement a protocol that shares it?

At stake: Whether the LVN can exist at all without buy-in from the companies that control the AI assistants. If it can't, is the initiative a beautiful theory that can never be built?
Pos AMCP changes the game. Anthropic's Model Context Protocol already enables inter-system context sharing. The trend is toward interoperability, not silos. Build the LVN as an MCP extension and adoption becomes a competitive advantage, not a sacrifice.
Pos BBuild on open-source AI. Don't depend on corporate AI. Build for Llama, Mistral, and local models. Let users run their own context engines. This is slower but captures no one's permission.
Pos CMake it too valuable to ignore. If the LVN generates measurable cooperative value for communities, commercial AI providers will implement it because their users demand it. The internet succeeded not because ISPs wanted openness, but because users demanded interoperability.
What's the minimum viable cooperative value that would make an AI company implement this over their competitive objections?
09
Is the Latent Value Gap real, or is it just friction that markets haven't solved yet?
EconomicsSocial Science

The LVN thesis is that a massive amount of cooperative potential goes unrealized due to informational barriers. But economists might counter: if the value were really there, markets would have found it. TaskRabbit, Nextdoor, and LinkedIn exist precisely to reduce cooperation friction. Maybe the "gap" is just cooperation that isn't actually worth the coordination cost.

At stake: The fundamental validity of the entire initiative. If the latent value gap is smaller than claimed, the LVN is a solution in search of a problem.
Pos AMarkets only capture articulable value. The gap exists precisely because it involves cooperation that people can't articulate as demand. You can't search for what you don't know you need. Markets require articulation; the gap IS the set of things that resist articulation.
Pos BThe evidence speaks. Chetty's 21 billion friendship analysis, Granovetter's weak ties, the organizational knowledge loss data — all quantify unrealized cooperative potential at massive scale. The gap is empirically documented across multiple domains.
Pos CMaybe the gap is real but AI matching isn't the solution. Perhaps the barrier is social, not informational. People know their neighbors exist; they lack the norms and social infrastructure to cooperate. The LVN treats a cultural problem as an information problem.
Describe one cooperation in your own life that almost didn't happen — and what it would have taken for it to happen automatically.
10
Who should build this first?
StrategyCommunity PracticeAll

The LVN needs a first pilot community. The wrong choice wastes years. The right choice proves the concept and generates the data needed to refine the protocol. The community must be small enough to iterate, diverse enough to test cross-boundary matching, and trust-rich enough to tolerate early failures.

At stake: The first real-world test of whether ambient cooperative matching works. If the pilot fails, it could discredit the concept for a generation.
Pos AA rural community with high latent value density. Small towns where people have diverse skills but limited economic opportunity. The gap is acutely felt and the cooperative culture may already exist. Think: Newton, Illinois, not San Francisco.
Pos BAn existing mutual aid network. They already have the cooperative norms and trust relationships. The LVN adds scale and discovery to what they're already doing. Lower adoption barrier because the cultural groundwork is laid.
Pos CA university or research community. Intellectually engaged, digitally literate, accustomed to collaboration, and generating tons of data about what works. Not representative of the general population, but ideal for rapid iteration.
If you had to pick one community of 500 people to test this with, who would they be and why?

These questions won't answer themselves.

Pick the provocation that annoys you the most — the one where you think we're wrong — and tell us why. That's how cooperative infrastructure gets designed.
